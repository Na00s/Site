Faynt: learning competitive Melee with one policy for 26 characters

Oct 2026 · Research article: Faynt at Frisson Labs · By Ali Janati and Nikita Kuzmin, with advisory contributions from Rohit Swamy and Charles Niu

Faynt is our study of learning competitive Super Smash Bros. Melee from human replays and gameplay. We train two Transformer policies, with 10M and 75M parameters, each controlling all 26 characters through one checkpoint. The work follows the full path from predicting a human controller input to choosing actions that win games, then making those decisions within a single frame.

The paper, by Ali Janati, Nikita Kuzmin, Rohit Swamy, and Charles Niu, brings together the architecture, training experiments, gameplay evaluations, and inference measurements. This walkthrough follows the main decisions along that path.

Training and architecture

A policy reads structured game state: the fighters, their positions and actions, the stage, and recent controller inputs. It predicts the next controller command. Training windows span 256 frames, about 4.3 seconds at Melee's 60 Hz. That history gives the model information about how a situation developed before it chooses the next input.

The dataset contains 839,942 accepted human replays and approximately 17.48 billion training targets. We split by complete game, keeping both players' perspectives together, so validation measures prediction on separate games. Pretraining uses behavioral cloning: increase the probability of the command the human player actually chose.

The backbone uses grouped-query attention and Full Attention Residuals, which learn how to combine earlier representations. Model-size and optimizer sweeps, together with measured throughput, guide the pretraining budgets. We use Muon for hidden backbone matrices and AdamW for the remaining parameters. The paper evaluates the resulting recipe; individual architecture components would need further controlled experiments to establish their contribution to playing strength.

Curriculum and distillation

The replay collection includes players of different skill levels and both sides of each match. Supervised post-training changes which demonstrations the policies see. The curriculum moves from the natural replay distribution toward winning play, then toward winners in the Master and Diamond ranks.

This also changes how we select checkpoints. We track a weighted prediction loss: 90% Master-winning loss plus 10% Diamond-winning loss. Later training uses approximately the same proportions of replay visits. Overall validation loss remains useful for tracking the broader distribution, while the weighted score follows the behavior emphasized by this stage.

The 10M policy then learns from the 75M teacher. Distillation gives equal weight to predicting the recorded human action and matching the teacher's probability distribution over commands. That distribution supplies information about plausible alternatives at each frame. Two rounds, with a lower learning rate in the second, reduce the 10M weighted loss from 0.76443 to 0.75746.

Gameplay provides a separate check. Across the same 152-game schedule, supervised post-training raises 10M wins from 49 to 106 and 75M wins from 69 to 123. The supervised 10M outperforms the pretrained 75M despite its higher overall prediction loss. The weighted score agrees with the win-rate ordering of these four checkpoints, a useful observation whose scope is this evaluated set.

Learning through play

Reinforcement learning continues from the supervised checkpoints using proximal policy optimization, or PPO. Both policies train in Fox mirror matches across six stages. Rewards include taking stocks and dealing damage, alongside movement, offstage, and ledge terms. Penalties on changes in action probabilities help keep updates controlled and preserve a connection to the supervised policy.

The 10M receives a second run using full self-play, with a longer reward horizon and several changes to the objective. Extending the reward half-life from four to eight seconds gives later consequences more weight. These settings change together, and the two model sizes have different training histories, so their final scores reflect the complete training paths.

Reading the gameplay results

The final 10M wins 240 of 244 same-character games against fourteen Slippi-AI releases on their supported characters. Those opponents retain 21- or 24-frame action delays, while Faynt uses zero added delay. The evaluation leaves the contribution of that timing difference unresolved.

A separate comparison uses one privately supplied Slippi-AI checkpoint with zero added delay, under Master Player and Cody conditioning. The final 10M wins all 68 games; the 75M wins 58. These results describe that opponent and schedule. Only Fox receives reinforcement-learning experience, while all 26 characters share the updated weights and appear in the expanded evaluation.

One decision per frame

Melee allows about 16.67 milliseconds per frame. The standard PyTorch path spends substantial time launching small operations and reading cache bookkeeping back to the host. Keeping that bookkeeping on the GPU and replaying most of the policy step through a CUDA graph cuts this overhead.

On recorded game inputs, optimized T4 inference averages 5.2 ms for 10M and 8.7 ms for 75M, with 99th-percentile latencies of 6.8 and 12.1 ms. The timings use random weights and include feature conversion, sampling, and command decoding; emulator execution and communication are excluded. Late decisions still occur, including 0.6% for T4/75M. A separate check with trained checkpoints reproduces the same sampled actions across 1,200 frames.
